    Then why does it feel like there are more?

    The number of earthquakes is not what you hear about. The news shows you the ones that caused serious damage, and that is not the same thing at all. Two earthquakes of the same magnitude can have completely different impacts.

    Where it happens
    Most earthquakes are under the ocean or under empty ground, and nobody notices. The same earthquake under a city is a catastrophe.
    How deep it is
    A magnitude 6 fifteen kilometers down can collapse buildings. The same magnitude 6 at two hundred kilometers down is barely felt.
    What the ground is made of
    Soft valley sediment shakes like jelly and amplifies the movement. Hard rock carries shaking much further, so many more people feel it.
    What people build
    The same shaking that a well-built city shrugs off will flatten unreinforced brick and concrete. This, more than the magnitude, decides how many people die.
    What else is nearby
    Shaking can crack a dam, breach a chemical plant or damage a nuclear station. That kind of harm outlasts the earthquake by years.
    What it sets off
    An earthquake under the sea can raise a tsunami that arrives minutes later. One in the mountains can shake a hillside down onto a village or a highway. Some of the deadliest earthquakes on record killed very few people by shaking alone.
    What time it is
    An earthquake at three in the morning catches people indoors and asleep. One at three in the afternoon catches them on trains, shopping, at school.

    And then there is you. There are twice as many people alive as in 1976, so the same earthquake is felt by twice as many of them. Nearly everyone now carries a camera, and footage of a collapsing building on the other side of the world reaches you within minutes. There aren't more earthquakes than there used to be. You are just seeing far more of them.
